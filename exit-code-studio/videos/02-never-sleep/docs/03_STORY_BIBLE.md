## 0. The story in one line

One ordinary Tuesday, humanity stops needing sleep — and over ten years, eight billion people discover that the hours they got back were never the gift; deciding who they belong to is.

## 1. The thought experiment

**The rules of this world** (ledger HR1–HR7):

1. From one day onward, **nobody needs sleep**. That includes babies born afterwards.
2. **Nobody ever gets tired** from being awake.
3. **Not sleeping causes no harm.** Whatever sleep did — the parts science knows and the parts it doesn't — now just happens.
4. **The body clock keeps running.** Melatonin still rises at night, temperature still dips before dawn, light still sets the clock. People feel the night; they are just never sleepy.
5. People can still lie down and close their eyes. They never fall asleep, so **nobody dreams**.
6. **Everything else is the same:** the sun still sets, solar panels still stop at night, people are still people.
7. **How it happens is never explained.** This is a thought experiment, not a prediction.

**The science device — "an hourglass and a clock."** Real sleep is run by two separate systems:
* **The hourglass:** sleep pressure. Adenosine builds up while you're awake; coffee blocks it.
* **The clock:** the circadian rhythm, set by light, signalling night with melatonin.

The breakthrough stops the hourglass **forever** and leaves the clock ticking. This keeps the brief's distinction visible all film long: *the need for sleep disappears; the biological night does not.*

## 2. Theme, tone and style

* **Theme:** time doesn't make a life — what you choose to do with it does. Sleep was the one pause everybody shared and nobody had to justify. Remove it, and every pause has to be chosen.
* **Tone:** a cinematic science documentary with a human heart. Wonder first, then consequences, then a quiet, earned ending. Balanced: the breakthrough brings real gains (learning, care, creativity, prosperity) as well as costs.
* **Escalation** (BRIEF): one family (ch. 3) → the world economy (4) → the city (5) → the human cost (6) → civilisation (7) → one person again (8).
* **Style:** Video #1's look — flat 2D, soft gradients, bloom, letterbox, grain — with the navy science-graphic style for chapter 2.
* **Colour script:**
  * **The 24-hour world:** navy and black with restrained teal and cool white (the thumbnail palette).
  * **Home:** warm amber pockets.
  * **The ending:** deep blue-black with silver stars.
  * **BLACK 24:** grey and cold teal neon.
* **Narration:** second person ("you" = Leo, as in Video #1) for the family thread; documentary voice for the world chapters. At the very end the "you" turns to the real viewer.

### Chapter 2 — The Biological Breakthrough (0:35–1:30)

*Brief: explain what sleep normally does — brain, memory, learning, metabolism, immunity, biological clocks; what the breakthrough would have to change; no invented mechanism presented as science.*

1. **Set-up** (Leo, 15, yawning over homework; Mia, 8, asleep with her notebook): "Ten years earlier, sleep wasn't optional. But what does it actually do?"
2. **The hourglass** (navy graphic: a brain holding an hourglass, sand made of tiny dots; a coffee cup caps it): "While you're awake, a chemical called adenosine builds up in your brain — sleep pressure. Coffee works by blocking it." (B01–B03)
3. **The clock** (a small clock inside the brain, light streaming in through the eyes, indigo melatonin at night): "And a clock deep in your brain, set by light, tells your body when it's night." (B04, B06)
4. **What sleep does** — three fast icons popping from a sleeping brain:
   * **Memory and learning** (a film strip replaying Leo's guitar chord and a maths problem): "Studies suggest the sleeping brain replays your day and helps lock in what you learned." (C01)
   * **Immunity** (virus icons bouncing off a rested body, slipping into a tired one): "In one study, people sleeping under six hours were about four times more likely to catch a cold." (C03)
   * **Metabolism** (sugar cubes and an "age" dial): "Six short nights left healthy young men handling sugar like much older ones." (C05)
5. **What nobody knows** (two mouse-lab cards stamped YES and NO; a big question mark over a dreaming brain): "And some of it, nobody understands. What REM sleep — home of the most vivid dreams — is for. Whether sleep really cleans the brain: some mouse studies say yes, others say no." (D02, C06 + C08 → D04)
   * *Optional, if the timing allows:* "A few rare families need only four to six hours. Nobody needs zero." (F03)
6. **What the breakthrough would have to change** (the hourglass freezes; the clock keeps ticking): "So a breakthrough would have to keep the hourglass empty forever — and do everything sleep does while you're awake. Even the parts we don't understand. How? Nobody knows. That's the thought experiment." (HR2, HR3, HR7)
7. **The rule card** (on screen, as in the reference's rule beat): "In this world: you never get tired. Skipping sleep never hurts you. But the clock keeps ticking." (HR4)

**Transition:** "Then, one ordinary Tuesday… the hourglass stops." The sand freezes in mid-air.

### Chapter 3 — The First 24 Hours (1:30–2:20)

*Brief: the first day; many additional waking hours; family, hobbies, education, sports, entertainment, personal projects; different ways people use the time.*

1. **Morning, Day 1 — the planted detail.** Mia draws in her notebook. "Every morning, Mia draws last night's dream." We glimpse stars and two tiny figures, nothing more.
2. **10 p.m.** "Nobody's tired. Not you. Not Mum. Not anyone." The family on the couch, wide awake. Pancakes at midnight. A board game at 1 a.m.
3. **1 a.m. — the window montage.** Each beat 1–1.5 s, each a different colour pocket in the night city:
   * a packed 24-hour gym (sports);
   * a game server crashing under record players (entertainment);
   * Grandma starting a painting (hobby);
   * a student opening a language course (education);
   * a dad and kid building a treehouse by headlamp (personal project);
   * a garage band (hobby);
   * one office window where a man in a grey suit is already booking 3 a.m. meetings (foreshadowing Mr. Black).
4. **Mia's question.** Mia in bed, eyes open: "Goodnight?" — then: "Leo… how do you dream if you don't sleep?" Leo has no answer. (opens L4)
5. **4 a.m. — the clock is still ticking.** A thermometer dipping, indigo melatonin, Leo wrapped in a blanket, wide awake. "Your body still knows it's night. Your temperature drops to its daily low. Melatonin is flowing. Outside, it's dark. You're just… not tired." (B06, B07, HR4)
6. **Sunrise.** "Twenty-four hours awake, and you feel fine. You just got eight hours back — and tomorrow you get eight more. That's 2,920 hours a year." (CALC-02)

**Transition:** "Eight billion people did the same maths. So did their bosses." Mr. Black smiles.

### Chapter 4 — The World Economy (2:20–3:20)

*Brief: continuous business operations, flexible shifts, night-time services, automation, changing employment patterns; who benefits and who loses; more hours ≠ more productivity or happiness.*

1. **The scale** (world map; crowd icons doubling on every continent): "Overnight, humanity gained the waking hours of about four billion extra people." (CALC-03)
2. **Continuous operations** (Mr. Black cuts the ribbon on **BLACK 24 — the company that never closes**): factories, warehouses and call centres stop closing. "Even today, about one in six US workers doesn't work regular daytime hours. In this world, the night shift is just… a shift." (G02, SP-01) Flexible rotas: "2 a.m. to 10 a.m. Or 6 p.m. to 2."
3. **Night-time services:** banks, dentists and government offices open at 3 a.m.; deliveries at 4 a.m.
4. **Automation and jobs:** "Machines already worked all night. Now people can too. Some jobs grow — anything people want at 3 a.m. Others vanish: mattresses, sleeping pills." (SP-02, SP-03)
5. **Winners and losers** (split cards):
   * **Winners:** businesses using buildings and machines around the clock; customers; anyone who wanted a second degree or a second job. And one cost disappears: tiredness, estimated today to cost the US economy up to $411 billion a year. (G01)
   * **Losers:** workers, because when everyone can work more, an hour of work can be worth less (SP-02). And the food bill: "awake bodies burn more — in one small study, a sleepless night cost about 135 extra calories. Across humanity, that's like feeding half a billion more people." (C09, CALC-04 — speculative)
6. **Hours ≠ output** (a curve rising, then flattening, ending in a question mark): "In First World War factories, past about 49 hours a week each extra hour added less — and 70 hours made barely more than 56. Those workers got tired. In this world nobody does… but nobody knows if people can stay focused twenty hours a day." (G03, D05)
7. **The personal thread.** Mum is offered BLACK 24's "flexible" night shift at 30 % more pay. She takes it. The family is better off — and Mum's chair at dinner is empty. (opens L6)

**Transition:** "The economy changed in weeks. Cities had to keep up."

### Chapter 5 — A City That Never Sleeps (3:20–4:20)

*Brief: public transport, restaurants, hospitals, schools, entertainment, infrastructure, energy consumption; do not assume every city runs 24 hours.*

1. **Transport** (a wide 3 a.m. city in full motion; trains every few minutes): "New York's subway already runs all night. Now every big city tries." (H01)
2. **Infrastructure** (a repair crew working beside a passing train, orange sparks): "But cities fix their trains and roads at night, when nobody's riding. In a city that never stops… when do you repair it?" (H08, SP-06)
3. **Restaurants and entertainment:** dinner at 3 a.m., concerts at 5 a.m., cinemas that never go dark.
4. **Hospitals:** "Hospitals were always open. Now 3 a.m. is as busy as noon." Planned operations around the clock.
5. **Schools:** three sessions — morning, evening, night (SP-07). Mia's class starts at 8 a.m.; Leo's at 9 p.m. *(This sets up chapter 6.)*
6. **Energy** (a grid graphic: night demand climbing while the solar line drops to zero; batteries, wind, dams and gas plants fill the gap): "Lighting already uses roughly 15 % of the world's electricity. Keep a city bright all night and demand jumps — just when solar panels produce nothing." (H02, H03, SP-08)
7. **The sky** (the stars fading; birds singing under streetlights; turtle hatchlings heading for the lights instead of the sea): "More than 80 % of people already live under light-polluted skies. Here, the stars go faster. Birds sing at the wrong hour. Turtle hatchlings crawl toward the streetlights instead of the sea." (H04, H06, SP-09)
8. **Not every city** (split screen: megacity glare | a small town dark under stars): "But not every city goes 24/7. Some can't afford it. Some don't want it. Small towns follow Flagstaff, Arizona — in 2001, the world's first International Dark Sky City — and switch the lights off at midnight. On purpose." (H07, SP-05)

**Transition:** "Cities adapted in a year. People took longer."

### Chapter 6 — The Hidden Cost (4:20–5:30)

*Brief: human consequences — more time together? relationships and routines? longer work? boundaries? happier? Balanced and scientifically honest.*

1. **The good first, honestly** (warm amber vignettes: a midnight family dinner, a 4 a.m. walk with friends, a parent reading to a child): "At first, people spent more time together."
2. **The split** (a 24-hour clock with three coloured arcs — Mum's night shift, Leo's night school, Mia's morning school — barely overlapping): "Then the schedules split. Your family is awake twenty-four hours a day… and almost never at the same time." (SP-10) "More time doesn't automatically mean more time together — even before the change, people were spending less time with friends." (G05)
3. **Boundaries** (a phone lights up at 3 a.m.: Mr. Black — *"You're up, right?"*): "For all of history, sleep was the one excuse nobody could argue with. 'Sorry, I was asleep.' Now nobody is." "Some countries already have right-to-disconnect laws — France since 2016, Australia since 2024 — because work was already leaking into our nights." (G06, SP-11)
4. **Happiness** (an inverted-U curve; at one end someone learning the cello or caring for a parent, at the other someone scrolling at 4 a.m.): "Would eight extra hours make us happier? Research suggests free time helps — up to about two hours a day. Past about five, well-being can actually dip… unless the time goes to something that matters. Some people flourish. Some just drift." (G04, SP-12)
5. **The quiet loss** (Mia, about 11, flipping through her notebook: one last dream drawing, then page after page left blank): "And then there's the loss nobody planned for. No sleep… no dreams. What were dreams for? Nobody knows. Now nobody has them." (SP-13, D03; closes L4)
6. **Signature line** (on screen, then voice): **"We didn't lose sleep. We lost the night."**

**Transition:** "Ten years later, this is the world we built."

### Chapter 7 — Ten Years Later (5:30–6:40)

*Brief: a cinematic hypothetical vision of civilisation ten years on; cities, services, businesses, routines; speculative, not a prediction; build toward the largest consequences.*

Framing: everything here is narrated as imagination — "Imagine it's ten years on…", "might", "could".

1. **Title card: TEN YEARS LATER.** "You're twenty-five."
2. **The planet** (Earth from orbit, the night side glowing like a second dawn): "From space, Earth's night side barely looks like night anymore." (SP-09)
3. **Routines** (the giant 3:00 clock over a jammed street — the callback to the hook): "3 a.m. is just another rush hour. Days don't end anymore. They change shifts." (SP-15)
4. **Cities and services:**
   * schools in three shifts;
   * 24-hour transit;
   * robots repairing tunnels between trains;
   * power plants that never idle;
   * everything always open. (SP-01, SP-06–SP-08)
5. **Business** (BLACK 24 logos everywhere): "The world economy is bigger. But for many people, the working week crept up, not down." (SP-11)
6. **Darkness becomes a luxury** (a coach of tourists in a dark-sky town, all looking up): "Darkness has become a luxury. People pay to visit towns that still switch off at night — just to see the stars." (SP-14)
7. **Night laws** (a parliament vote; a "quiet hours" sign): "Some countries guarantee quiet hours — when nobody can be called to work." (SP-11)
8. **The largest consequence** — the emotional peak and the hook's payoff (a classroom at 3 a.m.; the teacher describes dreams like ancient history; a child asks, "What did it feel like?"): "And there's a whole generation of children who have never had a single dream." (SP-13; closes L3)
   * *Optional (SP-16), only if it earns its seconds:* adverts everywhere for headsets that simulate dreams.
9. **The family now:** Leo plans night operations at BLACK 24 and is never off; Mum still works nights; Mia, 18, packs for a university in another city that runs around the clock. (closes L2)

**Transition:** "Which brings us back to 3 a.m."

### Chapter 8 — The Final Question (6:40–7:30)

*Brief: return to the individual; humanity has gained enormous waking time — what would people actually do with it? End with the line; the final moment must feel meaningful; no generic outro.*

1. **Crisis.** 3:00 a.m. — the hook's shot, now understood: Leo at the window, eyes open, the city glowing. His phone buzzes: Mr. Black, *"Promotion's yours. Starts tonight."*
2. **A knock.** Mia, 18, her bag packed for tomorrow, holding the old notebook: "Remember nights?" *(Optional: "Ten years. About twenty-nine thousand extra hours. Where did yours go?" — CALC-05)*
3. **The choice.** Leo looks at the message… and switches the phone off.
4. **The drive.** They drive out of the glow to a dark-sky town. The city's light falls behind them and the stars come out — thousands of them.
5. **The payoff.** On the hill, Mia opens the notebook to her last dream, drawn ten years ago: two figures on a hill under a sky full of stars. It is this moment. (closes L5)
6. **Goodnight.** They lie in the grass. Nobody sleeps. "Goodnight, Leo." "Goodnight, Mia." Voice: "For one night… they took it back." (closes L8)
7. **Back to reality** (the camera rises into the stars; the fiction ends): "Back in the real world, there's no breakthrough. Even the rarest short sleepers need four to six hours, most adults need seven or more — and science still can't fully explain why." (F03, A05, D01)
8. **Final line** (exactly as the brief): **"If you never had to sleep again… what would you do with the life you got back?"**
   * Hold on the stars, a beat of silence, the EXIT CODE mark. **No outro.**

## 8. Retention plan

* **Immediate payoff:** the opening image *is* the premise, and the thumbnail promise (eyes open, the 3 a.m. clock, the night city) is paid within 2 s.
* **Open loops are always running:** L1–L8 above. At least one stays open until the final minute: the notebook drawing, revealed only in chapter 8.
* **New information in every chapter:** 2 brings sleep science; 4 the scale of the economy and productivity research; 5 transit, energy and the sky; 6 happiness research and disconnect laws; 7 the world at scale.
* **Visual change at least every ~4 s** (Production Standard §8). Each chapter has a new set or graphic language.
* **Chapter transitions** are single bridging lines (listed above), not recaps.
* **No artificial suspense:** every promise in the hook is kept, in order.
* **No repetition:** the signature line is said once; chapter 8 only echoes it ("they took it back").
* **Emotional payoff:** the notebook drawing and "Goodnight" — the story's one quiet moment, after six minutes of acceleration.

## 9. Science guardrails for the script

* Every factual sentence has a claim ID. Research-supported findings say "studies suggest" or "in one study"; speculation says "might / could".
* **Never say:**
  * that scientists know every purpose of sleep (D01);
  * that REM sleep has one function (D02);
  * that "sleep cleans your brain" as fact (always C06 with C08 → D04);
  * that melatonin is "the sleep hormone".
* **Keep the two systems separate:** the hourglass stops; the clock keeps going.
* **The future is imagined:** chapter 7 is framed as imagination, never as prediction.
* **Responsible ending:** chapter 8 tells viewers plainly that real people need sleep (A05, F03).
